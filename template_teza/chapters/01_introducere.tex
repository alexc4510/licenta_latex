\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

In 2014, generating a photorealistic fake image required a skilled artist and hours of
manual work. By 2022, it required a text prompt and a few seconds of computation. The
gap between what is real and what is generated has narrowed to the point where it is no
longer reliably visible to the human eye, and in many cases is not visible at all.

The technology responsible for this shift falls into two main families. Generative
Adversarial Networks~\cite{goodfellow2014}, introduced by Goodfellow et al.\ in 2014,
train a generator and a discriminator in an adversarial loop: the generator attempts to
produce images the discriminator cannot distinguish from real ones, and the discriminator
attempts to tell them apart. StyleGAN~\cite{karras2019}, one of the most successful GAN
architectures, produces human portrait images so visually consistent that a gallery of
synthetic faces is indistinguishable from a stock photo library by casual inspection.
Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models~\cite{ho2020}, introduced in 2020, approach the
problem from the opposite direction: they learn to reverse a gradual noise process, and
at inference time produce high-quality images by iteratively denoising random Gaussian
noise. Latent Diffusion Models~\cite{rombach2022}, which operate the diffusion process in
a compressed latent space rather than pixel space, made this approach computationally
tractable at scale and produced the Stable Diffusion family of models now deployed in
products used by millions. Contemporary systems, including DALL-E~3, MidJourney~v6, and
SD~3, generate photorealistic images of arbitrary content from natural language
descriptions at resolutions and quality levels that are routinely indistinguishable from
photographs in controlled evaluations.

The social implications are substantial. AI-generated images have been used to fabricate
evidence in conflict reporting, to impersonate public figures in political propaganda, and
to produce misleading content at a scale that outpaces manual moderation. Synthetic images
have been deployed in disinformation campaigns during elections, where fabricated
photographs of candidates in compromising situations have circulated on social media
before fact-checkers could intervene. The speed of generation (seconds per image) and the
low barrier to access (free or low-cost API endpoints) mean that the volume of potentially
synthetic content entering platforms has grown faster than the capacity to label it.

In scientific publishing, fabricated experimental images present a different but equally
serious problem. Gel electrophoresis bands, microscopy images, and clinical photographs
can be generated or manipulated to show results that were never obtained. Automated
screening tools applied to submitted manuscripts have detected image duplication and
manipulation at rates of several percent in some journal samples, suggesting that the
problem is not rare. The introduction of generative models that can produce wholly
synthetic experimental images rather than manipulating real ones raises the stakes:
manipulation leaves detectable traces, whereas synthesis may not.

Social media platforms moderate billions of images per month; even a small false negative
rate on AI-generated content translates to a large absolute volume of synthetic material
reaching audiences without disclosure. Detection of AI-generated images is therefore a
practical problem with measurable consequences for information integrity, not a
theoretical benchmark exercise.

Automated detectors based on Convolutional Neural Networks approach the problem as a
binary classification task: given an image, assign it to the ``real'' class or the
``AI-generated'' class. CNN-based detectors have achieved strong in-distribution
performance, with macro-averaged F1-scores above 0.99 on balanced benchmarks involving
a single generator. The difficulty arises not in detecting the specific generator the
model was trained on, but in generalising to generators it has not seen. Wang
et al.~\cite{wang2020} showed that a CNN detector trained on one GAN architecture fails
on images from a different GAN, even within the same generation paradigm. The same
pattern appears across generation paradigms and dataset boundaries. A model that scores
F1=0.9961 on its own training distribution can score 0.1553 on a different one, as the
experiments in Chapter~4 confirm. The drop of 0.84~F1 between in-distribution and
out-of-distribution performance on the same model is not an edge case. It is the
central empirical observation that motivates this thesis.

The failure raises a specific question. ResNet architectures~\cite{he2016} are capable
of learning rich hierarchical representations from large labelled datasets. Their
in-distribution performance is near-ceiling. The collapse at test time cannot be
attributed simply to insufficient model capacity, because models with more parameters
fail in essentially the same way. The failure must therefore reflect a property of what
the models learn, not a limit on what they can represent. This leads directly to the
hypothesis that CNN detectors learn statistical properties specific to their training
dataset rather than features of the generation process itself.

\section{Research Question and Scope}
\label{sec:research_question}

This thesis investigates the cross-dataset generalisation behaviour of CNN-based AI image
detectors trained on three publicly available datasets spanning two generation paradigms:
diffusion models and a GAN. The central question is:

\begin{quote}
  \textit{Do CNN-based AI image detectors learn generative artefacts that transfer across
  datasets and generator families, or do they overfit to dataset-specific statistical
  shortcuts?}
\end{quote}

The investigation is empirical and systematic. Two architectures, ResNet-50 and ResNet-18,
are trained on each dataset individually and on progressively larger combinations of
datasets. Every trained model is evaluated on every test set, producing a 3$\times$3
cross-dataset generalisation matrix for each architecture. The training corpus is expanded
across four stages: from single-source training through a balanced variant, a combined
two-dataset corpus with a comparison of RGB versus Difference of Gaussians input, and a full 80,000-image multi-source
corpus with advanced regularisation. The progression is designed to isolate which training
modifications produce genuine improvements in OOD robustness, rather than artefacts of
in-distribution evaluation.

The scope is restricted to CNN-based detectors fine-tuned from ImageNet-pretrained
weights. More recent approaches, including the CLIP-based universal detector of Ojha
et al.~\cite{ojha2023}, address the generalisation problem through architectural changes
and frozen feature spaces. The CNN baseline studied here provides the reference point
against which such improvements are measured, and the experiments document the specific
failure modes that motivate them.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions_intro}

The main contributions of this thesis are the following.

\textbf{Quantitative 3$\times$3 cross-dataset generalisation matrix.} All nine
combinations of training dataset and test dataset are evaluated for both ResNet-50 and
ResNet-18. The matrix covers Dataset~A (Stable Diffusion~v1.5 / Shutterstock),
Dataset~B (five diffusion models / MS-COCO), and Dataset~C (StyleGAN / FFHQ), spanning
two generation paradigms (diffusion and GAN) and three independent real-image sources
(Shutterstock, MS-COCO, FFHQ). The minimum off-diagonal F1 is 0.1436; several cells
reach exactly 0.3333, the value produced by a classifier that assigns every input to a
single class. The matrix provides a fine-grained quantitative record of where
generalisation fails, across which paradigm boundary, and at what severity, for both
architectures simultaneously. Prior work covers multiple generators but within a single
real-image source; the three-source design here separates generator shift from real-image
source shift for the first time in a controlled multi-stage setting.

\textbf{Multi-stage training progression demonstrating data diversity as the key factor.}
Fourteen experiments across four stages trace the effect of each successive modification
on OOD performance. Stage~1 establishes single-source baselines (best OOD F1 on Dataset~A
of 0.6738). Stage~2 adds class-balance correction (marginal gain). Stage~3 combines two
datasets and tests DoG preprocessing (OOD F1 on Dataset~A rises to 0.7361). Stage~4
adds all three real-image sources with Focal Loss and extended augmentation (OOD F1 on
Dataset~A reaches 0.9936). Each improvement correlates with the addition of a new
real-image source, not with data volume alone. The controlled sequence isolates the
effect of each modification, which aggregate comparisons across systems cannot do.

\textbf{Per-generator breakdown of Dataset~B failure modes.} The Dataset~B test split is
partitioned by generator label using the per-sample metadata CSV, producing five
independent balanced evaluations (7,500 real + 7,500 AI each). This analysis reveals
that real recall is uniformly 0.61 across all five generator subsets, including
MidJourney~v6, which was excluded from training. The uniform floor is not visible in the
aggregate F1 of 0.5798, which conflates real-class errors with generator-class variation.
The per-generator methodology is applicable to any multi-generator benchmark with
provenance metadata and provides a diagnostic for the source of errors that aggregate
metrics do not offer.

\textbf{DoG preprocessing ablation.} Difference of Gaussians preprocessing
($\sigma_1=1.0$, $\sigma_2=2.0$) is evaluated against raw RGB as an alternative input
representation across two architectures and three test datasets. In all six comparisons,
RGB outperforms DoG: the losses are 0.005 F1 on Dataset~A, 0.011 on Dataset~B, and 0.013
on Dataset~C for ResNet-50. The result establishes that ImageNet-pretrained CNNs already
encode the high-frequency information that DoG is intended to expose, while DoG discards
colour and low-frequency content that contributes to classification. This finding rules
out spectral band-pass preprocessing as a path to improvement for standard fine-tuned
architectures.

\textbf{\texttt{dataset\_combined\_v2} multi-source training corpus.} An 80,000-image
balanced corpus is constructed from three datasets spanning two paradigms, with
MidJourney~v6 withheld as an unseen-generator probe. The corpus combines 23,000
Shutterstock photographs, 7,000 MS-COCO images, and 10,000 FFHQ portraits as real images,
paired with 40,000 AI images from six generator families. The construction methodology,
training configuration, and evaluation scripts are included in the appendix.

\section{Thesis Structure}
\label{sec:structure}

Chapter~2 reviews the literature relevant to this work. It covers the two main families
of generative models (GANs and diffusion models), CNN-based approaches to AI image
detection, the cross-dataset generalisation challenge, and the two most directly relevant
prior works: Wang et al.~\cite{wang2020} (CNNSpot) and Ojha et al.~\cite{ojha2023}
(universal fake image detector).

Chapter~3 describes the three datasets and all methodological choices: dataset acquisition
and preprocessing, combined corpus construction, the ResNet-18 and ResNet-50
architectures, training configurations for both the standard (Experiments~1--12) and
advanced (Experiments~13--14) setups, and the evaluation protocol including per-generator
test filtering.

Chapter~4 presents all 14 experiments in order of the four-stage progression. Each stage
introduces its modifications, reports training dynamics, and presents test results across
all three datasets. The chapter includes the full cross-dataset generalisation matrices,
per-experiment result tables, training curves, confusion matrices, and the per-generator
breakdown of Dataset~B.

Chapter~5 interprets the results. It argues for the dataset-specific shortcut hypothesis,
explains the three factors behind Dataset~B difficulty, traces the effect of data
diversity across stages, explains why DoG preprocessing fails, analyses the learning rate
scheduling benefit observed in Experiment~13, and examines the interaction between
architecture capacity and data scale.

Chapter~6 summarises the main findings, states the contributions, and proposes directions
for future work.

The appendix contains the Python source code for dataset construction, model training,
cross-dataset evaluation, and per-generator analysis, included as code listings for
reproducibility.
