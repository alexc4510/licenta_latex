\section{Datasets}
\label{sec:datasets}

Three publicly available datasets were selected to span two distinct generation paradigms and three
independent real image distributions. Covering both diffusion-based synthesis and generative
adversarial networks was essential for studying cross-dataset generalisation: a detector that
succeeds within one paradigm may fail entirely on the other. Equally important is the diversity of
real images: a model that overfits to the statistical properties of one real image source will
perform poorly when tested against photographs from a different source.
Table~\ref{tab:dataset_summary} summarises the key properties of each dataset.

\subsection{Dataset A --- AI vs.\ Human-Generated Images}
\label{sec:dataset_a}

Dataset A is a binary classification dataset available on Kaggle. It contains 79,950 training
images and 7,995 test images, with both splits balanced between classes: 39,975 real and 39,975
AI images in the training set, and 3,998 real and 3,997 AI images in the test set.

The real images are professional photographs obtained from Shutterstock, covering a broad range of
subjects including people, animals, landscapes, and objects. The AI-generated images were produced
by Stable Diffusion v1.5~\cite{rombach2022}, a latent diffusion model that generates images from
text prompts through iterative denoising in a compressed latent space. SD v1.5 outputs are
photorealistic and stylistically diverse.

All images were pre-resized to $224 \times 224$ pixels using a centre-crop operation and stored
in RGB format on Google Drive prior to training.

\subsection{Dataset B --- Defactify Image Dataset}
\label{sec:dataset_b}

Dataset B is the Defactify Image Dataset, available on HuggingFace. It covers five diffusion systems (Stable Diffusion 2.1, SDXL,
Stable Diffusion 3, DALL-E 3, and MidJourney v6) spanning multiple generator families and
capability levels. The real images are natural
photographs sourced from MS-COCO, covering everyday scenes such as kitchens, street scenes, and
outdoor environments.

The dataset is structured with semantic pairing: each real image is associated with five
AI-generated counterparts, one per generator, each produced from the same MS-COCO caption.
This pairing creates a direct semantic correspondence between the real and AI images, which
makes purely semantic shortcuts less exploitable by a detector.

The class distribution of the test set is imbalanced: 7,500 real images and 37,500
AI-generated images (7,500 per generator), yielding a 1:5 real-to-AI ratio. This imbalance
reflects the fact that each real image has five AI counterparts. Table~\ref{tab:dataset_b_splits}
shows the training subset used in this study.


A metadata CSV supplied with the dataset provides a \texttt{label\_b} column encoding the
generator identity of each AI image (1 = SD 2.1, 2 = SDXL, 3 = SD 3, 4 = DALL-E 3,
5 = MidJourney v6). This column was used in the per-generator evaluation described in
Section~\ref{sec:eval_protocol}.

The five generators in Dataset~B span a wide capability range as of 2024. SD~2.1 is a
second-generation latent diffusion model with improved text-image alignment relative to
SD~1.5. SDXL is a larger-scale variant with a two-stage refinement pipeline that
produces higher-resolution and more photorealistic outputs. SD~3 adopts a diffusion
transformer architecture with multi-modal text conditioning. DALL-E~3 is a commercial
system with proprietary architecture and safety filtering that tends to produce clean,
high-contrast outputs. MidJourney~v6 is the most stylistically distinct of the five:
its outputs have a characteristic aesthetic quality, high perceptual sharpness, and
deliberate compositional choices that differ visually from the other four. These
stylistic differences across generators are the source of the AI recall variation
(0.43--0.78) observed in the per-generator evaluation.

All images were pre-resized to $224 \times 224$ pixels and stored in RGB format.

\subsection{Dataset C --- 140k Real and Fake Faces}
\label{sec:dataset_c}

Dataset C is available on Kaggle and contains 140,000 face images: 100,000 in the training split
and 20,000 in the test split, with both splits balanced. The real images originate from the
Flickr-Faces-HQ (FFHQ) dataset, which consists of high-quality portrait photographs scraped from
Flickr. The AI-generated images were produced by StyleGAN~\cite{karras2019}, a GAN-based
architecture that generates photorealistic face images through adversarial training between a
generator network and a discriminator network.

Dataset C is the sole representative of the GAN generation paradigm in this study, in
contrast to the diffusion-based paradigms of Datasets~A and~B. Its domain is highly
specialised: all images are portrait faces with consistent framing, centre-aligned
subjects, and similar aspect ratios. The FFHQ real images are high-resolution
photographs downloaded from Flickr under permissive licences and aligned using facial
landmark detection, producing a dataset with low photographic diversity compared to
Shutterstock or MS-COCO.

This domain specificity has two consequences for detection. In-distribution, it makes
the task easier: a classifier can exploit face-specific artefacts in StyleGAN outputs
(ear lobe asymmetry, eye specular highlight inconsistency, background blurring
patterns) that are not present in the FFHQ real images. The in-distribution F1 scores
of 0.9978 (ResNet-50) and 0.9928 (ResNet-18) reflect this. Out-of-distribution, the
specialisation limits transferability: a model trained on aligned face images has no
exposure to the diverse subjects and compositions of Shutterstock or MS-COCO, and the
learned representation fails to generalise.

Images were pre-resized to $224 \times 224$ pixels and stored in RGB format.

\section{Combined Datasets}
\label{sec:combined_datasets}

Two combined datasets were constructed to study the effect of training data diversity on
cross-dataset generalisation.

\textbf{dataset\_combined (Experiments 9--12).} This dataset merges training subsets from Datasets
B and C. It contains 28,000 training images: 7,000 real and 7,000 AI from Dataset B (1,400
images per generator across five generators), and 7,000 real and 7,000 AI from Dataset C. The
resulting corpus is evenly split between diffusion-generated and GAN-generated AI images.
The motivation was to test whether mixing two paradigms already improves out-of-distribution
generalisation to Dataset A, which was held out entirely. No dedicated test split was created;
models were evaluated on the original test sets of Datasets A, B, and C.

\textbf{dataset\_combined\_v2 (Experiments 13--14).} The second combined dataset merges all three
source datasets into an 80,000-image training corpus: 40,000 real images and 40,000 AI images.

\section{Model Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

Two ResNet architectures~\cite{he2016} were used throughout all experiments: ResNet-18 and
ResNet-50.

ResNet (Residual Network) is a convolutional neural network family introduced by He et al.\ to
enable training of very deep networks by addressing the vanishing gradient problem. The core idea
is the residual block: instead of learning a direct mapping from input $\mathbf{x}$ to output
$\mathbf{y}$, the network learns a residual function $\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x})$ and adds the
original input via a shortcut connection:

\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{y} = \mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}, \{W_i\}) + \mathbf{x}
  \label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}

When the dimensions of $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$ differ (across downsampling stages), the
shortcut is implemented with a $1 \times 1$ convolution. This identity path ensures that
gradients can flow directly to early layers regardless of network depth, making deep networks
trainable without degradation.

The two architectures differ in the type of residual block used:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{ResNet-18} uses \textit{basic blocks}: each block contains two $3 \times 3$
        convolutions with batch normalisation and ReLU activation. The network has four block
        groups with (2, 2, 2, 2) blocks respectively, and 512 feature maps before the final
        fully connected layer.
  \item \textbf{ResNet-50} uses \textit{bottleneck blocks}: each block contains a $1 \times 1$
        convolution to reduce dimensionality, a $3 \times 3$ convolution, and another
        $1 \times 1$ convolution to restore dimensionality. The four block groups contain
        (3, 4, 6, 3) blocks, and there are 2048 feature maps before the final fully connected
        layer.
\end{itemize}

Table~\ref{tab:architectures} summarises the key properties of each model.

\begin{table}[tb]
  \centering
  \caption{Comparison of the two ResNet architectures used in this study.}
  \label{tab:architectures}
  \begin{tabular}{lll}
    \hline
    \textbf{Property} & \textbf{ResNet-18} & \textbf{ResNet-50} \\
    \hline
    Total layers        & 18               & 50 \\
    Block type          & Basic ($3{\times}3$, $3{\times}3$) & Bottleneck ($1{\times}1$, $3{\times}3$, $1{\times}1$) \\
    Block groups        & (2, 2, 2, 2)     & (3, 4, 6, 3) \\
    Feature dim (pre-FC) & 512             & 2048 \\
    Parameters          & $\approx$11.7M  & $\approx$25.6M \\
    \hline
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Both models were initialised with weights pretrained on ImageNet, a large-scale visual
recognition dataset containing over 1.2 million images across 1,000 categories. Transfer
learning from ImageNet is standard practice: the convolutional layers encode general visual
features (edges, textures, shapes, and increasingly abstract patterns at deeper layers)
that transfer well to new visual classification tasks. Fine-tuning on the target dataset adapts
these features to the binary real-vs-AI task.

The final fully connected layer of each model was replaced to output two classes instead of the
original 1,000 ImageNet classes. Class labels follow the alphabetical order imposed by PyTorch's
\texttt{ImageFolder}: \texttt{ai} $= 0$, \texttt{real} $= 1$.

\section{Preprocessing}
\label{sec:preprocessing}

\subsection{Standard Preprocessing}
\label{sec:standard_preprocessing}

All images across all three datasets were pre-resized to $224 \times 224$ pixels using a
centre-crop operation and converted to RGB. Pre-resizing was performed offline and stored to
Google Drive before training, eliminating repeated resizing overhead during data loading.

During training and evaluation, images were normalised using ImageNet channel statistics:

\begin{equation}
  \hat{x}_c = \frac{x_c - \mu_c}{\sigma_c}
  \label{eq:normalisation}
\end{equation}

where $x_c$ is the raw pixel value in channel $c \in \{\text{R}, \text{G}, \text{B}\}$,
$\mu = [0.485,\, 0.456,\, 0.406]$, and $\sigma = [0.229,\, 0.224,\, 0.225]$. These statistics
were computed from the ImageNet training set and are standard for models pretrained on ImageNet,
as they ensure the input distribution at inference time matches the distribution seen during
pretraining.

\subsection{Difference of Gaussians Preprocessing (Experiments 11--12)}
\label{sec:dog_preprocessing}

In Experiments 11 and 12, an alternative input representation was evaluated: the Difference of
Gaussians (DoG) transform, applied to the greyscale conversion of each image before feeding it
to the model.

The DoG is computed as:

\begin{equation}
  \text{DoG}(I) = G(I,\, \sigma_1) - G(I,\, \sigma_2)
  \label{eq:dog}
\end{equation}

where $G(I, \sigma)$ denotes convolution of image $I$ with a Gaussian kernel of standard
deviation $\sigma$, and $\sigma_1 < \sigma_2$. With $\sigma_1 = 1.0$ and $\sigma_2 = 2.0$, the
DoG acts as a band-pass filter, suppressing both very low-frequency content (large-scale
structure, colour) and very high-frequency noise, while emphasising edges and fine textures in
the intermediate frequency band. The output is normalised to $[0,\,1]$ and replicated across
three channels to produce a $224 \times 224 \times 3$ input compatible with the model.

This preprocessing targets characteristic upsampling artefacts produced by generative models,
particularly GANs, which manifest as periodic spectral peaks in the high-frequency domain.
Filtering out colour and low-frequency content was expected to expose these artefacts more
directly to the classifier. The results of this preprocessing are presented in Chapter~4.

\subsection{Standard Configuration (Experiments 1--12)}
\label{sec:standard_config}

Experiments 1 through 12 used a common training configuration.

\textbf{Loss function.} Cross-entropy loss was used with inverse-frequency class weights to
compensate for class imbalance. The weight for class $c$ was set to:

\begin{equation}
  w_c = \frac{N}{C \cdot n_c}
  \label{eq:class_weight}
\end{equation}

where $N$ is the total number of training samples, $C$ is the number of classes, and $n_c$ is
the number of samples in class $c$. For balanced datasets (A and C), these weights are equal.
For Dataset B, the real class receives a weight five times higher than the AI class,
counteracting the 1:5 training imbalance.

\textbf{Optimiser.} Adam with a learning rate of $\eta = 10^{-4}$ and default momentum
parameters ($\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.999$). Batch size was 128.

\textbf{Preprocessing and augmentation.} Standard preprocessing as described in
Section~\ref{sec:standard_preprocessing} was applied to all inputs. The only data augmentation
during training was random horizontal flipping with probability 0.5. No augmentation was applied
at evaluation time.

\textbf{Checkpoint selection.} Model weights were saved at each epoch. The checkpoint with the
highest validation accuracy was selected as the best model for final test evaluation.

The choice of Adam with $\eta = 10^{-4}$ reflects standard practice for fine-tuning
ImageNet-pretrained ResNets on binary classification tasks. Adam's adaptive per-parameter
learning rates accelerate convergence on the final classification layer, which begins with
randomly initialised weights, while applying smaller effective updates to the pre-trained
convolutional layers whose weights are already near their optimal values for feature
extraction. The ten-epoch maximum is sufficient because ImageNet pretraining substantially
reduces the required adaptation time: all Stage~1 models converge within four epochs, with
no meaningful improvement observed in later epochs. Batch size~128 was the largest that
fit within the 16~GB VRAM budget of the NVIDIA T4 GPU at $224\times224$ resolution.

\subsection{Advanced Configuration (Experiments 13--14)}
\label{sec:advanced_config}

Experiments 13 and 14 used an extended configuration designed to improve generalisation on the
larger and more diverse dataset\_combined\_v2.

\textbf{Loss function.} Cross-entropy was replaced by Focal Loss~\cite{lin2017}:

\begin{equation}
  \text{FL}(p_t) = -(1 - p_t)^{\gamma} \log(p_t)
  \label{eq:focal_loss}
\end{equation}

where $p_t$ is the model's estimated probability for the correct class and $\gamma$ is the
focusing parameter, set to $\gamma = 2.0$. Focal Loss down-weights easy examples (those the
model classifies with high confidence) and concentrates training on hard misclassifications.
Label smoothing with $\varepsilon = 0.1$ was also applied, replacing the hard one-hot targets
$(1, 0)$ with soft labels $(1 - \varepsilon, \varepsilon / (C-1))$ to prevent overconfident
predictions and improve calibration.

Focal Loss was preferred over the inverse-frequency weighted cross-entropy used in
Experiments~1--12 for two reasons. First, weighted cross-entropy addresses class imbalance
at the distribution level by scaling all samples of the minority class equally, but does
not distinguish between easy and hard examples within that class. Focal Loss applies an
additional per-sample weight $(1 - p_t)^\gamma$ that is large when the model is uncertain
($p_t$ near 0.5) and small when the model is confident, regardless of class membership.
On a multi-source training corpus where some image subsets are consistently easier than
others, this per-sample weighting prevents the easy subsets from dominating the gradient
signal. Second, the larger and more diverse Stage~4 corpus makes overconfident predictions
more likely: the model encounters many easy in-distribution examples alongside genuinely
hard cross-source examples. Label smoothing with $\varepsilon = 0.1$ reduces overconfidence
by preventing the model from assigning probability near 1.0 to any single class.

\textbf{Data augmentation.} The augmentation pipeline was extended relative to the standard
configuration. Table~\ref{tab:augmentation} summarises the transforms used in each
configuration and their parameters. All augmentations are applied only during training;
no augmentation is applied at validation or test time.

\begin{table}[htb]
  \centering
  \caption{Data augmentation transforms for standard and advanced training configurations.}
  \label{tab:augmentation}
  \begin{tabular}{lllp{5cm}}
    \hline
    \textbf{Transform} & \textbf{Standard} & \textbf{Advanced} & \textbf{Rationale} \\
    \hline
    RandomHorizontalFlip & Yes ($p=0.5$) & Yes ($p=0.5$) & Left-right symmetry holds for all three datasets \\
    ColorJitter          & No            & Yes            & Prevents exploitation of colour statistics specific to each real-image source \\
    RandomRotation       & No            & Yes ($\pm10^\circ$) & Robustness to slight orientation differences across distributions \\
    RandomErasing        & No            & Yes ($p=0.2$)  & Discourages reliance on a single localised region; promotes distributed evidence \\
    \hline
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Learning rate schedule.} A linear warmup was applied over the first two epochs,
linearly increasing the learning rate from 0 to $\eta = 10^{-4}$. After warmup, a
\texttt{ReduceLROnPlateau} scheduler monitored validation F1-score and reduced the learning
rate by a factor of 0.5 when it did not improve for 3 consecutive epochs. The minimum learning
rate was $10^{-6}$.

\textbf{Early stopping.} Training was halted when validation F1-score did not improve for 5
consecutive epochs. The maximum number of training epochs was set to 20.

\textbf{Gradient clipping.} Gradient norms were clipped to a maximum of 1.0 per parameter to
prevent instability in early epochs when the warmup LR is still low.

\textbf{Checkpoint selection.} For Experiments 13 and 14, the checkpoint with the highest
validation F1-score was used. This change from the standard configuration aligns checkpoint
selection with the primary evaluation metric.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:eval_protocol}

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

The primary evaluation metric throughout all experiments is the macro-averaged F1-score. Let
$\text{TP}_c$, $\text{FP}_c$, and $\text{FN}_c$ denote the true positives, false positives, and
false negatives for class $c$ respectively. The per-class precision and recall are:

\begin{equation}
  P_c = \frac{\text{TP}_c}{\text{TP}_c + \text{FP}_c},
  \qquad
  R_c = \frac{\text{TP}_c}{\text{TP}_c + \text{FN}_c}
  \label{eq:pr}
\end{equation}

The per-class F1-score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall:

\begin{equation}
  F1_c = \frac{2 \cdot P_c \cdot R_c}{P_c + R_c}
        = \frac{2\,\text{TP}_c}{2\,\text{TP}_c + \text{FP}_c + \text{FN}_c}
  \label{eq:f1}
\end{equation}

The macro-averaged F1-score averages $F1_c$ uniformly over all classes, regardless of class
frequency:

\begin{equation}
  F1_{\text{macro}} = \frac{1}{C} \sum_{c=1}^{C} F1_c
  \label{eq:macro_f1}
\end{equation}

With $C = 2$ (AI and real), macro F1 gives equal weight to both classes. This is the appropriate
metric for imbalanced datasets such as the Dataset B test set (1:5 imbalance): accuracy alone
would overstate performance by favouring the majority AI class, whereas macro F1 penalises
disproportionate failures on either class.

\subsection{In-Distribution Evaluation}
\label{sec:indist_eval}

For experiments with a dedicated test split (Experiments 1--8), the best checkpoint was
evaluated on the corresponding test set. Reported metrics include macro accuracy, macro
precision, macro recall, macro F1-score, a per-class breakdown, and a confusion matrix.

\subsection{Cross-Dataset Evaluation}
\label{sec:crossdataset_eval}

For all Stage~1 models (Experiments 1--6), each best checkpoint was additionally evaluated on
the test sets of all three datasets, regardless of which dataset it was trained on. This
produces a $3 \times 3$ cross-dataset generalisation matrix where entry $(i, j)$ is the macro
F1-score of the model trained on dataset $i$ and tested on dataset $j$:

\begin{equation}
  M_{ij} = F1_{\text{macro}}\bigl(\theta_i^*,\; \mathcal{D}_j^{\text{test}}\bigr)
  \label{eq:generalisation_matrix}
\end{equation}

where $\theta_i^*$ denotes the best checkpoint of the model trained on dataset $i$. The diagonal
entries ($M_{ii}$) represent in-distribution performance; the off-diagonal entries quantify
generalisation to unseen data distributions.

\subsection{Combined Dataset Evaluation}
\label{sec:combined_eval}

For Experiments 9--14, where no dedicated test split was constructed, models were evaluated
directly on the original test sets of Datasets A, B, and C. For the combined models this
evaluates both in-distribution (datasets present in training) and out-of-distribution
(Experiments 9--12, where Dataset A was never seen during training) performance.

\subsection{Per-Generator Evaluation}
\label{sec:pergenerator_eval}

The Dataset B test set contains 7,500 AI images from each of the five generators, paired with
7,500 real images per subset. The metadata CSV was used to filter the test set by
\texttt{label\_b}, producing five subsets (7,500 real + 7,500 AI from one generator each).
Each checkpoint was evaluated separately on each subset, yielding per-generator accuracy and
macro F1-score. This evaluation was performed for Experiments~13 and~14 only, since they
represent the final combined training configuration.

The balanced per-generator subsets differ from the full Dataset~B test set (7,500 real +
37,500 AI) in one important respect: the 1:5 class imbalance is resolved. Macro F1-scores
computed on a balanced subset give equal weight to both classes, making per-generator
comparisons directly interpretable. On the imbalanced full test set, the aggregate macro
F1 of 0.5798 is depressed primarily by real-class errors rather than by AI-class errors;
the per-generator breakdown is the correct diagnostic for identifying which generators
drive poor performance, because it separates real-class errors (which are consistent across
generators) from AI-class errors (which vary by generator). The two evaluations are
complementary: the aggregate F1 reflects overall deployment-level performance, while the
per-generator analysis identifies the specific failure modes.

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:implementation}

All experiments were implemented in Python using PyTorch 2.3.0 and torchvision 0.18.0. Training
was performed on a Google Colab environment with an NVIDIA T4 GPU (16~GB VRAM). Datasets were
stored on Google Drive, with all images pre-resized to $224 \times 224$ pixels before training
to eliminate repeated resizing overhead during data loading.

File enumeration in the DataLoader was implemented using \texttt{os.listdir} rather than
Python's \texttt{glob} module. The FUSE driver used by the Google Drive filesystem produced
incomplete file listings when glob patterns were used, causing random training samples to be
silently dropped. The \texttt{os.listdir} approach was verified to enumerate all files
correctly.

cuDNN benchmark mode was enabled (\texttt{torch.backends.cudnn.benchmark = True}), allowing
cuDNN to select optimised convolution algorithms for the fixed $224 \times 224$ input size.
Two DataLoader worker processes with a prefetch factor of 2 were used to overlap data loading
with GPU computation, maintaining high GPU utilisation throughout training.

The checkpoint system saved model weights, optimiser state, and training metadata (current
epoch, best validation metric, early stopping counter) at each epoch, allowing training to be
resumed after runtime interruptions without loss of progress.

Colab runtime interruptions were a practical constraint throughout the project. Each Colab
session has a maximum duration after which the runtime is recycled and all in-memory state
is lost. Training was designed to tolerate this: on every session start, the training script
checks for an existing checkpoint in the output directory on Google Drive and resumes from
the last completed epoch if one is found. For all experiments except Experiment~14, this
mechanism operated correctly. In Experiment~14, a runtime crash at epoch~3 overwrote the
checkpoint of a previously completed run rather than resuming from it, because the session
was inadvertently launched without confirming the target output path. The experiment was

The 16~GB VRAM ceiling of the T4 constrained the maximum feasible batch size to 128 images
at $224\times224$ resolution with a ResNet-50 backbone. Larger batch sizes were not
attempted. In practice, GPU memory was the binding constraint for all Stage~4 experiments;
the combined dataset with Focal Loss and extended augmentation brought VRAM usage to
approximately 14~GB during forward passes at batch size 128.
