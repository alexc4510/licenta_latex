\section{The Dataset-Specific Shortcut Hypothesis}
\label{sec:shortcut_hypothesis}

The central finding of Stage~1 is that all six single-source models achieve near-perfect
in-distribution F1-score, but collapse almost completely when tested on data from a different
dataset. The minimum off-diagonal F1 is 0.1436 and two cells reach $\approx 0.33$,
the value expected from a model that predicts one class for the entire test set. This
pattern holds regardless of whether the generator is a GAN (Dataset~C) or a diffusion
model (Datasets~A and~B), and regardless of whether the model is ResNet-50 or ResNet-18
(Table~\ref{tab:cross_dataset_matrix}).

Figure~\ref{fig:matrix_ch5} shows the cross-dataset F1 matrix for ResNet-50. The
diagonal entries are near~1.0 for Datasets~A and~C and around~0.65 for Dataset~B.
Five of the six off-diagonal cells are below~0.40, except for the model trained on
Dataset~B and tested on Dataset~A. The matrix for ResNet-18 presents more hopeful
results (Figure~\ref{fig:matrix_ch5_r18}).

             in-distribution. Off-diagonal: OOD transfer.}

The consistency across architectures is the key piece of evidence. ResNet-50 has
approximately 2.2 times the parameters of ResNet-18, yet their off-diagonal F1 values
do not differ by a large margin. If generalisation failure were primarily
an issue of model capacity, the larger architecture would show systematically better
off-diagonal performance. It does not show a considerable one. The failure is therefore not architectural but
data-driven: both models learn shortcuts, and the shortcuts are the problem.

The most plausible explanation is that models learn dataset-specific shortcuts~\cite{geirhos2020shortcut}, rather
than generative artefacts. Each training dataset pairs AI images from a specific generator
with real images from a specific source. Dataset~A pairs Stable Diffusion~v1.5 outputs
with Shutterstock photographs; Dataset~C pairs StyleGAN faces with FFHQ portraits;
Dataset~B pairs five diffusion models with MS-COCO images. The classifier can achieve
near-perfect in-distribution accuracy by learning statistical properties that differ
between the specific real and AI sources in that dataset, without needing to learn anything
about generative artefacts as such. When tested on a different dataset, those
source-specific statistics no longer discriminate the two classes and performance
collapses.

Candidate shortcuts include: colour and luminance statistics specific to each real image
source (Shutterstock stock photography vs. FFHQ portrait faces vs. MS-COCO natural
images), JPEG compression profiles that differ between real and AI images, resolution
and sharpening patterns introduced by each generator's upsampling pipeline and aspect
ratio or cropping conventions. Wang et al.~\cite{wang2020} documented this same
shortcut-learning behaviour in CNN-based detectors trained on GAN-generated images, where
models trained on one GAN fail on images from others. The results here extend that
finding to diffusion models and to a three-dataset cross-paradigm setting.

Figures~\ref{fig:precision_matrix_ch5} and~\ref{fig:recall_matrix_ch5} show the
corresponding precision and recall matrices for ResNet-50. The recall matrix is
consistent with single-class prediction: when a model assigns every input to one class
on a balanced test set, one class achieves recall 1.0 and the other achieves 0.0, so
macro recall averages to 0.50. The cells with F1~$\approx 0.33$ all show macro recall near
0.50 (0.4927--0.5000), confirming the collapse interpretation. The precision matrix
shows values near 0.25 for those same cells: predicting a single class on a balanced
binary set gives precision 0.50 for the predicted class and undefined for the other,
averaging to approximately 0.25 under macro averaging. Together the three matrices
(F1, precision, recall) provide a complete picture of where the classifier collapses
and in which direction.

The $\approx 0.33$ floor observed in two off-diagonal cells (A$\rightarrow$C for both
architectures) requires closer examination. On a balanced binary test set, a macro F1
of $\approx 0.33$ is produced by a model that assigns every sample to the same class:
the predicted class achieves F1 $= 2/3$ (precision $= 0.5$, recall $= 1.0$), while the
unpredicted class achieves F1 $= 0$, and their macro average is $(2/3 + 0)/2 = 1/3$. 
The implication is not merely that the
model performs poorly on the new distribution: it is that the features learned during
training are actively misleading on it. A model trained on Dataset~A and tested on
Dataset~C encounters images from a completely different domain (Shutterstock photographs
vs. StyleGAN synthetic faces), and its learned shortcut predicts ``real'' for essentially
every input, because nothing in the new distribution matches the AI signature it was
trained to recognise. This is a qualitatively stronger statement than low F1: the
classifier has inverted the problem rather than simply become less confident. Ojha
et al.~\cite{ojha2023} identified this class of failure as the central motivation for
building architecture-agnostic universal detectors. The results here confirm that standard
fine-tuned CNNs remain vulnerable to it.

\section{Why Dataset B is the Hardest Target}
\label{sec:why_b}

Dataset~B consistently produces the lowest test F1 across all stages, including Stage~4
where the model was explicitly trained on Dataset~B images. Three compounding factors
explain this.

\textbf{Factor 1: Generator diversity.} Dataset~B contains five generators with distinct
visual characteristics: SD~2.1, SDXL, SD~3, DALL-E~3, and MidJourney~v6. Each generator
produces images with different texture statistics, colour palettes, and spatial frequency
profiles. A single model must learn one unified set of features that discriminates real
from AI across all five families simultaneously. This is harder than the single-generator
setting of Datasets~A and~C, where one consistent artefact signature dominates.

\textbf{Factor 2: MS-COCO real image underrepresentation.} In
\texttt{dataset\_combined\_v2}, the real images come from three sources: 23,000 from
Dataset~A's Shutterstock photographs, 10,000 from Dataset~C's FFHQ faces, and 7,000
from Dataset~B's MS-COCO subset. MS-COCO thus contributes only 17.5\% of the 40,000
training real images. The model's internal representation of ``real'' is dominated by
Shutterstock and FFHQ statistics. When evaluated on Dataset~B's test set, whose real
images are all MS-COCO, many are assigned to the AI class because they do not match the
dominant real-image distribution seen during training.

\textbf{Factor 3: Test set imbalance.} The Dataset~B test set contains 37,500~AI images
and 7,500~real images (1:5 ratio). Macro F1 averages the per-class F1 equally, so the
real class contributes 50\% of the final metric despite being 17\% of the test set.
Any systematic difficulty with the real class therefore depresses the aggregate macro~F1
disproportionately.

Understanding why MS-COCO real images are hard requires comparing the three real-image
sources. Shutterstock photographs are commercially curated stock images: consistent
white-balance, sharp subject focus, low noise, frequently centre-weighted composition.
FFHQ portraits are aligned face images with uniform framing and skin-tone normalisation.
MS-COCO images are natural-scene photographs drawn from Flickr, with cluttered
backgrounds, variable lighting, motion blur, and a wide range of subjects and viewpoints.
These two properties, visual diversity and lower curation, push MS-COCO images outside
the statistical region that the model has learned to label as ``real''. The model
effectively treats cluttered natural-scene photographs as uncertain, and given the
imbalanced training signal, defaults to the AI class.

The per-generator breakdown provides the decisive evidence for Factor~2 being the
dominant cause. Table~\ref{tab:per_generator_ch5} and Figure~\ref{fig:per_gen_ch5}
show the per-generator F1 and real recall for Experiment~13 (ResNet-50). Real recall
is 0.61 across all five subsets, including MidJourney~v6, which was excluded from
training.

\section{Effect of Data Diversity}
\label{sec:data_diversity}

The four experimental stages form a natural progression in training data diversity.
Table~\ref{tab:ood_progression} tracks the best out-of-distribution F1 on Dataset~A
(the primary OOD probe) and on Dataset~B across stages, using the ResNet-50 results.

\begin{table}[h!]
  \centering
  \caption{Best ResNet-50 out-of-distribution F1 per experimental stage. Dataset~A is
           never seen during training in Stages~1--3. Dataset~B is never seen during
           training in Stage~1 (for models trained on A or C).}
  \label{tab:ood_progression}
  \begin{tabular}{clcc}
    \hline
    \textbf{Stage} & \textbf{Training corpus} & \textbf{Best F1 on A} & \textbf{Best F1 on B} \\
    \hline
    1 & Single source (B only, for A column) & 0.6738 & 0.6512 \\
    2 & Dataset B balanced               & 0.6738 & 0.6711 \\
    3 & Dataset B + C (28k)              & 0.7361 & 0.6312 \\
    4 & Dataset A + B + C (80k)          & 0.9936 & 0.5798 \\
    \hline
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The pattern has a simple explanation: a model can only recognise real images from sources
it has seen during training. In Stage~1, each model sees real images from a single source,
so it fails when test real images come from a different one. Adding more real-image sources
to training directly fixes this. The large jump in Dataset~A F1 from Stage~3 to Stage~4
(0.7361 $\to$ 0.9936) confirms this: Stage~4 adds Shutterstock photographs to training,
which is the exact source of Dataset~A real images.

This means the Stage~4 improvement on Dataset~A is partly an in-distribution effect, not
a purely OOD one. Dataset~A real images are present in the Stage~4 training corpus. The
near-perfect F1 of 0.9936 on Dataset~A reflects a model that has seen the exact
real-image source, not one that generalises from unseen sources. The genuinely OOD
measurement for Stage~4 is Dataset~B, where MS-COCO real images remain
underrepresented.

Each stage that adds a new real-image source improves OOD robustness on that source's
distribution. Stage~3 adds FFHQ real images alongside MS-COCO, and F1 on the completely
unseen Dataset~A rises from 0.6738 to 0.7361 ($+0.062$). Stage~4 adds Shutterstock real
images, and F1 on Dataset~A jumps to 0.9936. The improvement is not primarily from more
data in total: it comes from more diverse real-image sources. Each new source narrows the
gap between what the model has seen as ``real'' and what appears in the test set.

Figure~\ref{fig:exp09_confusion_ch5} shows the confusion matrix for the Stage~3
ResNet-50 model (Experiment~9) on Dataset~A. Even without any Shutterstock training
images, the model correctly classifies a majority of both classes. The error structure is
symmetric: misclassification rates are comparable in both directions, unlike the strongly
asymmetric errors seen in Stage~1 (where the model assigned nearly all inputs to a single
class). This symmetry is consistent with a model that has learned a partial but meaningful
real-image representation, placing some Dataset~A images correctly on the real side of the
decision boundary.

\section{DoG Preprocessing Failure}
\label{sec:dog_failure}

Experiments~11 and~12 applied Difference of Gaussians preprocessing to suppress
low-frequency content and colour, with the goal of making generative upsampling artefacts
more visible to the classifier. The results in Table~\ref{tab:stage3_results} show
consistent degradation relative to the RGB baseline: DoG reduces F1 by 0.005 on
Dataset~A, 0.011 on Dataset~B, and 0.013 on Dataset~C for ResNet-50.

Two mechanisms explain this failure. First, ResNet-50 and ResNet-18 trained on RGB
already learn edge and high-frequency texture features through their early convolutional
layers. The first convolutional layer of a ResNet, initialised from ImageNet pretraining,
already acts as a bank of frequency-selective filters covering a range that overlaps with
the DoG band-pass. Applying DoG as a preprocessing step provides no additional
frequency-domain information that the network could not have extracted from RGB.

Second, and more importantly, the DoG transform discards colour and low-frequency content.
The preceding analysis suggests that the shortcuts models rely on for in-distribution
detection include colour statistics and low-frequency structure specific to each real-image
source. Removing this information eliminates a substantial part of the signal the model
uses for classification, which outweighs any benefit from artefact emphasis.

A more targeted approach for future work would be to add the FFT magnitude spectrum as
an additional input channel alongside RGB, rather than replacing the RGB input. This would
preserve colour and low-frequency content while providing explicit spectral information
alongside it.

\section{Adaptive Learning Rate Scheduling}
\label{sec:lr_scheduling}

Experiment~13 provides a clear example of ReduceLROnPlateau improving convergence. At
epoch~8, validation F1 reaches 0.9910, then fails to improve for three consecutive epochs
(9, 10, 11). The scheduler halves the learning rate from $10^{-4}$ to $5\times10^{-5}$
at epoch~12. Over the following two epochs, validation F1 rises to 0.9928 (epoch~13) and
0.9933 (epoch~14), the best checkpoint. The total gain attributable to the LR reduction
is $+0.0023$~F1 over two epochs.

Figure~\ref{fig:f1_curve_ch5} reproduces the validation F1 curve for Experiment~13.
The plateau in epochs~8--11 is visible as a flat region before the LR reduction at
epoch~12 triggers the final improvement. The shape is characteristic of
ReduceLROnPlateau: a period of no progress, a step reduction, rapid improvement over
one to two epochs, and then a new plateau at a slightly lower loss.

With learning rate $10^{-4}$, the optimiser overshoots the local minimum in the 
loss surface repeatedly, therefore the resulting oscillation is
visible in epochs~6--11 of Figure~\ref{fig:f1_curve_ch5}. Halving the step size to
$5\times10^{-5}$ reduces the overshoot and allows the model to settle into a narrower,
lower minimum. This is the standard motivation for adaptive LR scheduling and is observed
here with clear quantitative evidence.

The second LR reduction at epoch~18 (to $2.5\times10^{-5}$) produces no improvement.
By this point the model has likely reached a local minimum where further fine-tuning of
the learning rate does not help, and early stopping terminates training at epoch~19.

\section{Architecture Capacity and Data Scale}
\label{sec:capacity_data_scale}

The relationship between architecture size and generalisation performance changes
qualitatively as training data grows. At small data scale (Stage~1, single-source
training), ResNet-18 occasionally outperforms ResNet-50 in OOD transfer. Trained on
Dataset~B and tested on Dataset~C, ResNet-18 achieves F1 of 0.5019 versus 0.3997 for
ResNet-50. At this scale, the smaller model generalises better: it is likely that
ResNet-50 overfits the training distribution more tightly, memorising source-specific
features that are even less transferable than the coarser features learned by ResNet-18.

At large data scale (Stage~4, 80,000 images from three datasets), the relationship
inverts. ResNet-50 outperforms ResNet-18 consistently across all three test sets: by
0.0162 on Dataset~A (0.9936 vs.\ 0.9774), 0.0284 on Dataset~B (0.5798 vs.\ 0.5514),
and 0.0124 on Dataset~C (0.9987 vs.\ 0.9863). The larger model benefits more from the
richer and more diverse training signal. With 80,000 training images
spanning three real-image sources and six generator families, the dataset contains enough
signal to exploit ResNet-50's additional capacity without causing it to overfit any single
source.

This transition, from ResNet-18 parity or advantage at small scale to consistent ResNet-50
advantage at large scale, is a self-contained finding. The practical implication is that
architecture choice and dataset scale are not independent decisions: the larger model is
the better choice when a diverse, multi-source training set is available, but may
underperform the smaller model when only a single-source dataset is used.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Several limitations bound the conclusions that can be drawn from this study.

\textbf{MidJourney v6 exclusion.} MidJourney~v6 was excluded from
\texttt{dataset\_combined\_v2} and reserved as an unseen-generator probe. The model's F1
of 0.5192 on the balanced MidJourney~v6 subset confirms it cannot reliably detect this
generator. Any deployment scenario where MidJourney~v6 images are expected would require
including them in training.

\textbf{MS-COCO underrepresentation.} With only 17.5\% of training real images drawn
from MS-COCO, the model performs poorly on MS-COCO real images at test time. A rebalanced
training set with equal proportions from each real source would likely improve Dataset~B
real recall substantially.

\textbf{No dedicated test split for combined datasets.} The combined training sets
have no reserved test split and the evaluation is performed on the original held-out sets of
Datasets~A, B, and~C. For Stages~3 and~4, the training corpus overlaps with the source
distributions of the test sets, which may slightly overestimate performance on those
distributions relative to a fully independent test set.

\textbf{Restricted generator coverage.} The study covers six generator families:
Stable Diffusion~v1.5, SD~2.1, SDXL, SD~3, DALL-E~3, MidJourney~v6, and StyleGAN.
Generators not represented in any training or test set (e.g., Imagen, Adobe Firefly,
Flux, or future models) are outside the scope of these conclusions. Given the rate at
which new generators are released, detector performance on unseen generators is likely
to degrade over time.

\textbf{Rapidly evolving landscape.} AI image generation is advancing quickly. The
generators used here were current as of 2024. More recent models with
improved upsampling pipelines and higher perceptual quality may produce fewer of the
low-level artefacts that current CNN-based detectors rely on. This would further reduce
detection accuracy.

\textbf{Training environment constraints.} All experiments were run on a single NVIDIA
T4 GPU (16~GB VRAM) within Google Colab, which imposes practical limits on
reproducibility and training scale. Colab runtime interruptions caused one training run
(Experiment~14) to crash at epoch~3 of 20, overwriting the checkpoint written by a
previous full run. The final Experiment~14 results come from a complete re-run, but the
training curves and per-generator bar chart from the first run are corrupted and excluded
from Chapter~4. The 16~GB VRAM ceiling constrained the maximum batch size to 128 images
at $224\times224$ resolution. Larger batch sizes, which generally smooth the loss
landscape and improve generalisation, were not feasible without GPU upgrades. Experiments
at a larger scale, or with architectures such as ResNet-101 (${\approx}45$M parameters),
would require a multi-GPU or cloud training environment beyond the chosen Colab hardware,
increasing the costs considerably.
